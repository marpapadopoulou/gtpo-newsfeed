Return to Basics: Why Execution Matters More Than Strategy

International Banker 02 Sep 2026 7 min read
Change Management Processes AI
Key Insight

The real transformation gap in banking is not strategy — it is the absence of execution discipline and accountability for outcomes; executives should audit whether previous waves (digital, operating model redesign) were actually embedded before accelerating into AI.

Full Article

Over the last two decades, the traditional banking sector has not evolved gradually so much as shifted from one strategic priority to the next. Operationally, post-crisis regulatory reform gave way to digital transformation, which broadened into enterprise-wide digitization and operating-model redesign. Today, artificial intelligence (AI) has taken center stage.

Each wave has reshaped banking in meaningful ways, and each has rightly commanded management's attention. The problem often, is that institutions have repeatedly moved on to the next imperative before fully embedding the previous one.

This article argues that the industry's competitive advantage lies in shifting its focus from strategy formulation to execution discipline—i.e., from deciding what to do to consistently delivering on what has already been decided.

Banking has entered an era of perpetual strategic change.

The industry has moved through a series of distinct multi-disciplinary transformation waves:

- Post-crisis regulatory compliance,

- Digital transformation,

- Enterprise digitization and operating-model redesign,

- Environmental, social and governance (ESG),

- Artificial intelligence.

Each was, and is, in its own right, a legitimate strategic priority. Taken together, however, they have produced a cycle in which organizations continually redefine strategy while implementation struggles to keep pace.

A recent Dubai International Financial Centre (DIFC) report describes banking as undergoing its most significant structural shift since the 2008 financial crisis, with resilience—rather than scale—increasingly determining long-term success. The same report notes that AI is fast becoming core infrastructure rather than simply another technology initiative.

Strategy has become an event, while execution has become an afterthought.

Boardrooms devote considerable time to strategy development, transformation roadmaps and future-state operating models. Boards focus on three- and five-year ambitions, while far less attention is given to the detail required in Year One to make those ambitions achievable. Even less attention is paid to implementation discipline and accountability for missed outcomes.

More often than not, banks move straight into the next strategic refresh rather than sufficiently addressing issues such as:

- Have priorities actually been delivered?

- Have the expected benefits been realized?

- Has behavior changed?

- Has the operating model reached the "desired" stage?

- Was the return on investment (ROI) achieved from these initiatives?

- What has been done/needs to be done about the lack of results?

Richard Rumelt, author of Good Strategy/Bad Strategy, draws a similar distinction, describing good strategy as a coherent set of analyses, concepts, policies, arguments and actions—with the emphasis squarely on actions, not aspirations alone.

JPMorgan Chase offers a useful illustration of what treating implementation as a permanent habit rather than an annual event looks like in practice. Management disclosed at the bank's 2023 Investor Day that it has held technology spending at roughly 10 percent of revenue over several consecutive years, even as overall technology spending grew to a reported $18 billion for 2025. The strategy itself is unremarkable: Maintain consistent investments rather than increasing and reducing spending with each successive transformation cycle. What is notable is the discipline of sustaining it year after year—through post-crisis reform, digital banking and now AI—without resetting the clock each time a new imperative arrives.

Banks increasingly confuse ambition with capacity.

One of the most persistent weaknesses in strategic planning is treating organizational ambition as if it were organizational capability. Banks routinely launch several enterprise initiatives simultaneously—such as AI, data, customer experience, cost transformation, cloud migration, ESG and digital channels; each is individually justifiable, but collectively they often go beyond what the organization can realistically execute.

The scarce resource is rarely ideas. It is execution capacity.

This echoes Rumelt's broader point: Effective strategy requires focusing on the critical challenge rather than pursuing every opportunity simultaneously.

Consultants should challenge thinking, not replace it.

External advisors can add real value, but banks should be mindful of why they are engaged. They most certainly bring an outside perspective, specialist expertise, implementation experience and independent challenge. But ownership of strategy should remain firmly with management. No external adviser understands an institution's customers, culture, regulatory environment and operational constraints better than its own leadership team.

The most effective engagements are those in which management sets the strategic direction and advisors test assumptions, challenge biases and strengthen execution rather than providing the strategy. That rarely produces the optimal outcome.

Advisors should have "skin in the game", with compensation increasingly linked to outcomes rather than hours. This is not simply a preference; it is the direction in which the industry itself is moving. Ideally, the external team should also possess real operating experience in the business on which they are advising; clients are increasingly demanding this in practice. Private-equity firms, for instance, have spent two decades building in-house operating-partner functions for this reason; as one industry veteran put it, outside consultants who lacked "skin in the game" simply "didn't do a lot for us".

AI risks repeating the same cycle.

Artificial intelligence may be the most significant technological shift the financial-services sector has seen. Yet many institutions remain focused on AI pilots, use cases and centers of excellence rather than measuring what actually matters: customer outcomes, productivity gains, cost reductions, revenue growth and risk reduction.

AI demands greater implementation discipline than any transformation before it. Its success, at an institutional level, will depend far less on the technology itself than on the institution's ability to implement it effectively. As DIFC notes, AI is becoming foundational infrastructure, but resilience will ultimately hinge on how quickly and effectively banks adapt their operating models to it.

JPMorgan Chase's approach to its AI program is a good example of measuring outcomes rather than activity. Rather than reporting on pilot counts, the bank has built the internal financial discipline to attribute measurable benefits to each AI initiative before it is deployed and again once it reaches production, with its chief analytics officer describing the gross AI-attributed benefit as having grown at roughly 30 to 40 percent per year since the program's inception. The number itself matters less than the underlying habit: Benefit is tracked as rigorously as spend.

Execution will become the next competitive advantage.

The institutions most likely to outperform over the next decade will be those that demonstrate greater execution discipline—prioritization over proliferation, a realistic assessment of organizational capacity, governance built around delivery and management attention focused on implementation rather than continual redesign.

Ray Dalio, founder of Bridgewater Associates, has observed that the biggest mistake most people make is failing to see themselves and others objectively. Applied to strategy, this means honestly assessing what an organization can realistically deliver before deciding what it should pursue.

DBS Group is a clear illustration of what this looks like when it works. From a digital perspective, DBS owes its success less to technology than to disciplined execution. Former Chief Executive Officer Piyush Gupta deliberately spent the bank's early transformation years fixing core processes, technology foundations and organizational culture before launching broader digital initiatives. The result was not simply a digital strategy, but an institution capable of consistently delivering on strategic priorities for more than a decade.

What execution discipline actually looks like

Recognizing the problem is the easy part. Building the framework and the determined mindset to address it is harder, and it tends to rest on a small number of concrete practices rather than a single grand initiative.

A hard ceiling on concurrent priorities: Rather than running every justified initiative at once, disciplined institutions cap the number of enterprise-wide programs underway at any time and force genuine trade-offs between them. Saying no to a good idea is treated as a sign of maturity, not a failure of ambition.

Delivery and strategy metrics have the same visibility: Boards that track transformation spending and milestones just as closely as they track strategic vision tend to catch slippage early. Benefits realization, adoption rates and behavioral change become standing agenda items, not year-end retrospectives.

Named accountability for outcomes, not activities: Execution discipline requires a single owner accountable for a business outcome: a cost ratio, a customer metric, a risk reduction, rather than a program manager accountable for hitting a project milestone. The distinction matters: Milestones can be met, while the underlying benefit never materializes.

Operating-model changes that outlast the initiative: Many transformations fail to stick because they exist alongside the old operating model rather than replacing it. Institutions with genuine execution discipline change reporting lines, incentive structures and decision rights—not just processes and tools—so that the new way of working becomes the only way of working.

A deliberate slowing of the strategy cycle: Perhaps counterintuitively, execution-led institutions often refresh strategy less frequently than their peers, not because their ambition is lower, but because they treat the strategy as a commitment to be delivered rather than a document to be revisited.

None of this is novel management theory. It is part of the existing wisdom. What distinguishes the institutions that pull it off is less the sophistication of the practice and more the consistency with which it is applied day after day, week after week, month after month, well beyond the point at which the initial enthusiasm for a new strategic priority has faded.

The differentiator is discipline, not direction.

Most banks now have access to a broadly similar strategic playbook: the same regulatory obligations, the same AI vendors, the same consulting firms and the same peer benchmarks. Strategic direction, in other words, is becoming increasingly commoditized.

What remains genuinely scarce and genuinely differentiating is the organizational capacity to deliver on that direction consistently, year after year, without losing focus at the first sign of the next transformative technology or regulatory shift.

The institutions that internalize this will not necessarily have the boldest strategies. They will simply be the institutions that consistently deliver on agreed priorities while remaining pragmatic, aware of the challenges ahead and focused on what they can realistically execute—pragmatically, with eyes to the future and feet grounded on the earth.
